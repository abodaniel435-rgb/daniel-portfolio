Playbook - for the buyer with no laboratory

Seven checks,
forty seconds, one decision.

You are the sensor here. The inspector scores what you see, so the value is in the sequence: what you look at first, what each look actually proves, and the four answers that mean do not use it however friendly the shop is.

    01 · The sequence

    Seven looks, in the order that catches the most

    Do not shuffle this. The number and the date are free and need no light; the seams and the foil need thirty seconds of handling; the pack itself needs the pharmacist to be watching. Each line says what it proves and what it cannot prove.

    1. Read the registration number, before you ask the price

      Eight seconds. Find the number, then read the letter, the digit count and the dash. A- for an imported finished product, F for a pack made in Nigeria, five or six digits in the body, a dash between them, and no CR or X suffix on a retail carton.

      Proves: the string is a documented shape. Cannot prove: that this product is registered, or that the pack came from that registration.

    2. Read the date and the legend beside it

      Eight seconds. Find the letters next to the digits: EXP, USE BY, BEST BEFORE. Then resolve the order. A four-digit year with a month name is unambiguous; two digits under thirteen could be a month or a day, and you act on the earlier reading.

      Proves: which day the manufacturer stops standing behind the pack. Cannot prove: that the pack was stored correctly until that day.

    3. Look at the carton flap and the glue

      Six seconds. Run a thumb along the flap. You want one machine glue line at the factory seam. Clear tape over a flap, a lifted edge, a crushed corner with fresh glue, or a seam glued by hand is a re-packed carton and it is a fatal answer in this tool.

      Proves: whether somebody has already opened this box. This is the single highest-value look at a counter, and it is free.

    4. Look at the foil, up to the light

      Six seconds. The foil should be continuous, aligned with the cavities, and printed on both faces. Look for punched-through cells, a second layer of foil pasted over the first, and print that does not line up across the strip. Loose tablets inside a strip are a re-pack signal.

      Proves: whether the individual dose units have been tampered with or rebuilt. Cannot prove: what is inside a well-sealed cavity.

    5. Rub the batch and expiry with your thumb

      Four seconds. Machine inkjet, laser and embossed marks stay put. A hand ink-stamp printed over other text smears, sits at a slight angle, or rubs off on your thumb. Where a stamped date overprints a machine date, the stamped one is the lie.

      Proves: who printed the date, which is the closest a buyer gets to proof of a rebuilt pack. Fatal answer if it smears.

    6. Look at the medicine itself

      Five seconds. Across one strip the deboss, score line, shape and colour should be identical; no chipped, oversized or two-toned tablets, no powder dusting in a capsule blister. A syrup should be uniform with no hard cake at the base and an intact tamper band. An injection should be clear, the ampoule or vial uncracked, and the closure dry.

      Proves: that the units look like each other and like the last pack you bought of this brand. Cannot prove: that it contains anything at all, which is why passing here never proves authenticity.

    7. Ask for the receipt, and name the pharmacist

      Three seconds, and you do it while paying. An invoice or receipt naming the product and the batch, from a licensed outlet, is the fourth documentation check and the only one that costs the seller something. Check that the storage line matches what you are about to do with the pack: below 25 C, 2-8 C, protect from light.

      Proves: that there is a paper trail and a named premises. A refusal to write the batch on the receipt is information.

    02 · The traps

    Eight things that make a fake pass a lazy buyer

    Each of these has already fooled somebody at a counter. They are why a high score is permission to ask, never proof of safety.

    • A real registration number, printed here. Every format rule on the counter is public, so a genuine number taken from another product, another strength or a dormant listing passes the shape test perfectly.
    • A barcode from a genuine pack. Copy the bars from a real carton and the check digit arithmetic comes out right on every counterfeit printed afterwards. A passing digit proves almost nothing; a failing digit is close to conclusive.
    • A hologram sticker bought by the sheet. Colour-shift tiles are a purchasable item. They only mean something when the glue seam, the foil and the print quality agree with them.
    • A correct leaflet in the wrong carton. Leaflets are folded and printed in bulk. A strength in the leaflet that does not match the carton is a re-pack tell; a matching leaflet proves nothing at all.
    • A stolen deboss punch. Tablets can carry the right mark, the right score line and the right colour while carrying the wrong quantity of API. The product checks are the weakest ones on the list.
    • A real expiry date, on a pack that has been hot. The date is printed on the carton, not on the antigen inside it. A dark VVM and a perfect date can sit on the same vial, and the dial is the honest record.
    • Same brand, so it must be the affected batch. Notices name a batch or a batch range. A non-matching batch does not make your pack safe, and this tool's sample table is fiction either way.
    • The shopkeeper is a known name. Trust in the outlet is not a check. Honest chemists are sold re-packed consignments by their own distributors and do not know it, which is exactly what the seam and foil looks are for.

    03 · Before you pay

    The counter checklist

    Take forty seconds. Every line here has saved somebody a course of treatment that did not work.

    • You can read a number, and it is A- or F- with five or six digits and a dash. No CR or X suffix on a sealed retail pack, and nothing that is only four digits.
    • You have resolved the date and know which reading is earlier. Say it out loud to the seller: which month, which year. Ambiguity is resolved against use, not in your favour.
    • The carton flap has one machine glue line and no tape. If the flap lifts or crushes, you are holding a re-packed carton.
    • The foil is continuous, aligned and printed on both faces. No punched cells, no second layer, no loose tablets.
    • The date does not smear when you rub it. Machine print stays; hand stamps do not.
    • The units match each other across the strip. Same deboss, same colour, no dusting, no merged softgels, no cake in the bottle.
    • For anything cold-chain, you read the dial and the clock. VVM stage, and how many days since the syrup was reconstituted or the pen first used.
    • You have a receipt naming the product and the batch. Write the batch on it yourself if the seller will not. Keep the carton; do not accept a pack with no date printed on it.

    04 · When to walk away

    Leave it on the shelf and go to a licensed pharmacist

    These are the situations where the arithmetic stops mattering. In each one the cost of walking away is a bus fare and a few naira; the cost of buying is a failed course of treatment.

    Any number

    The number is missing, illegible, or not a documented shape

    The registration check is fatal for a reason. A four-digit body, a missing dash, or a B- prefix is not a transcription problem you should solve on the seller's behalf.

    Ask for the number of this exact product and strength. If it cannot be produced, buy nothing.

    The moment you see it

    The flap is re-glued, taped or crushed

    Someone opened this carton and closed it again. Do not put it back for the next buyer to find; tell the pharmacist at the premises what you found.

    This answer overrides the score: the tool returns do not use, report it.

    If it smears

    The date lifts off on your thumb

    A hand-stamped expiry means the date was chosen by whoever packed it. It is the cheapest falsification tell and the most commonly ignored.

    Every cold-chain item

    The VVM is at stage 3 or 4, or nobody can say how long it has been out

    Stage 3 and 4 discard on the dial alone, whatever the printed date says. Stage 2 means use within the session and discard at the end of it.

    For a vaccine this goes to the state EPI officer, not to a patient.

    For injections

    Anything in the solution is visible, or the closure is not dry

    Particles, a grey precipitate after freezing, a cracked neck, dried residue or weeping at the crimp. Sterility is not negotiable at a counter and there is no test you can run on it.

    Injections and IV fluids are a hard-stop class: the date governs, not your judgement.

    For a child

    Any doubt at all, on malaria, antibiotics, ORS or insulin

    A substandard antimalarial does not just fail the child; it selects for resistance that the next buyer pays for. Walk out, go to a licensed pharmacist, and come back with the pack examined in daylight.

    An unused medicine is a loss of money. A falsified one can be a loss of a child.

    If there is no receipt

    The outlet will not name itself on paper

    Without a receipt naming the product and the batch there is nothing to report and no premises to report. Buy elsewhere, and tell the pharmacist why you came back empty handed.

    05 · Questions buyers ask at the counter

    Straight answers, no hedging

    The number looks right. Is the medicine real?

    No, that is not what a valid shape tells you. The formats are public, so they are printable. What a valid shape buys you is the right to stop asking questions about the number; the seams, the foil, the date and the register are still open. Confirm the registration against this exact product, strength and pack, with a pharmacist present.

    Can I just ask the seller to check it for me?

    Ask, but do not rely on it. A licensed pharmacist or superintendent can test the number from the shop and carries the legal duty to quarantine stock, so involve them. An unlicensed seller in a market stall has neither the register nor the duty.

    The date says 04/27. What if the pack is actually out of date?

    Read it as end of April 2027, then check the alternative: 27 April of some year with no year printed is a different finding entirely. Where two readings are possible the earlier one governs, because the failure mode is a dead child rather than a wasted one thousand eight hundred naira.

    It is only one month past expiry. Can I still take it?

    For an injection, an antibiotic syrup, ORS, insulin or a vaccine: no, that class is a hard stop and the date is the limit, not a guideline. For a sealed tablet pack stored correctly, it is a conversation with a pharmacist rather than an automatic discard, and the printed date is still the limit.

    The syrup was mixed last week. Does the printed expiry still apply?

    Not once water is added. The clock on a reconstituted antibiotic suspension is 7 to 14 days refrigerated, whichever the insert says, and a carton date of 2027 is irrelevant after that. Ask the day it was mixed and write it on the bottle.

    The tablets look exactly like the ones I took last year. Is that enough?

    Look-alike cartons and tablets pressed with a copied punch can match in deboss, shape and colour while carrying little or no active ingredient. The product checks are the weakest on the list, which is why passing them never proves authenticity.

    What do I do with a pack I have already paid for and found defective?

    Do not finish it to be sure and do not return it to the shelf. Keep the pack, carton, leaflet and receipt, photograph every face plus the seal damage, and hand it to the pharmacist at the outlet and your state drug officer. Patterns are how investigations start, and a regulator can do nothing with a description.

    Is the score worth anything if everything passed?

    It is worth the discipline that produced it: coverage, in seconds, of weight you actually answered rather than guessed at. Six unsure answers score higher than four defects and describe a worse pack, so answer honestly and treat 85 or above as permission to keep the receipt, not as proof.

    06 · Words on the carton

    Glossary

    NAFDAC registration number
    The number printed on a registered product. Its shape is documented and copyable; its status lives only on the official register, which this tool never queries.
    A- prefix
    Imported finished product, registered through a Nigerian agent.
    F- prefix
    Finished product manufactured in Nigeria. An F- number on a pack describing an import is a mismatch worth asking about.
    CR and X suffixes
    Contract or repack-related registration, and bulk material or API registration. Neither belongs on a sealed retail carton.
    Legacy number
    The older plain numeric form, often written nn/nnnn. A recognised shape, but the listing behind it may be dormant or withdrawn.
    Blister foil
    The sealed cover over each dose cavity. Continuous, aligned and printed on both faces on a genuine pack; punched through or doubled over on a rebuilt one.
    Machine glue line
    The single bead of factory glue at the carton seam. Its replacement by clear tape, hand glue or a lifted flap is a re-packed carton.
    Batch print methods
    Inkjet, laser and emboss are applied by the manufacturer. A hand ink-stamp printed over other text smears with a thumb and is a fatal answer here.
    Deboss and score line
    The mark and the break line pressed into a tablet. They should match across the strip, and a punch can be stolen, so matching marks never prove content.
    GS1 prefix
    The leading digits of a barcode, pointing to the country where the licence holder was assigned a number block. Not the country of manufacture.
    Check digit
    The final barcode digit, computed mod 10 from the rest. A failed digit means mistyped, misread or badly copied; a passing one proves very little.
    Safe reading
    The earliest of the possible interpretations of a printed date. Ambiguity is resolved against use.
    Hard stop
    A class where the printed date or the in-use clock ends the product: injections and IV fluids, reconstituted antibiotic syrup, ORS plus zinc, vaccines, insulin.
    VVM stage
    The printed chemical disc on a vaccine vial that darkens with accumulated heat. Stage 2 means use within the session; stage 3 or 4 discards on the dial alone.
    Degree-hour
    Degrees off the storage band multiplied by hours at that temperature, summed across events. Excursions accumulate; they do not reset.
    Override or red flag
    An answer that fails the pack regardless of the arithmetic: a re-glued seam, a stamped batch, an unreadable number, particles in an injection, a dark dial, an out-of-date reading.
    Coverage
    The share of the 100 check weight you actually answered. Below 100 percent the score is provisional, because unanswered checks are excluded rather than credited.
    Patent medicine store
    The roadside outlet where most of these checks matter most and where the register is least likely to be open.
