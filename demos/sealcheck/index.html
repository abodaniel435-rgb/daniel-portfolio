Read this before anything else

No app on a phone can authenticate a medicine.

Only the regulator's register and a laboratory can say a product is genuine. A perfectly valid NAFDAC number can be printed on a falsified carton — the number is text, and text copies. What this tool does is catch the visible failures (a re-glued seam, an ink-stamped date, a swollen syrup bottle, a VVM that has turned dark) and force the questions that a buyer in a hurry does not get to ask. Treat a high score as permission to ask, never as proof of safety.

It can

Decode number formats, barcode prefixes and ambiguous dates; score a documented physical inspection; compute cold-chain degree-hours; draft a report with your observations in it.

It cannot

Verify the register, assay an API, detect a look-alike carton printed on the same machine, or tell you a tablet is active. Nothing here replaces discarding a doubtful pack.

When in doubt

Do not take it. Take the pack and the receipt to a licensed pharmacist, and report it. An unused medicine is a loss of money; a falsified one can be a loss of a child.

01 · The problem

Four people, four packs, no way to check.

A mother buys amoxicillin syrup for a fever at a patent medicine store in Onitsha. A student in Ibadan pays for “malaria drugs” at a chemist who has never heard of the register. A pharmacist in Benin City signs for a consignment whose carton seams are sticky. A village health worker walks insulin and vaccines to a PHC with no power and no ice.

All four are being asked to trust a piece of printed card. The checks that separate a genuine pack from a re-packed one take under two minutes and cost nothing — and almost nobody is taught them.

    03 · Registration numbers

    What the number says — and what it cannot say.

    NAFDAC registration numbers follow documented shapes. Learn the shape and most lazy fakes fall out immediately: the letter is wrong, the digit count is wrong, the dash is missing, or the suffix that means “bulk material” is printed on a consumer carton.

    Check a number

    Spaces and case are ignored the way an inspector reads them. Try A-45678, F-21348, 12/4567, A45678, A-1234567.

    04 · Barcode prefix

    600–601 is South Africa. Your “Made in Nigeria” carton says otherwise.

    A GS1 prefix tells you where the licence was issued — not where the goods were made, and certainly not whether they are genuine. It is still worth 20 seconds, because counterfeiters routinely copy a barcode without thinking about the prefix, and because the check digit is arithmetic that cannot be faked by accident.

    Digits only. Try 6141234567892 (Nigeria prefix), 8901234567890 (India), 6154234567892 (reported as Kenya), 50660209 (EAN-8 short code), 6141234567895 (check digit broken).

    Three honest limits

    • A prefix is the country of the licence holder. Nigerian-packaged goods with an Indian or Chinese prefix are common and legal.
    • Precious card stock is the weak point: prefixes, batch blocks and foil are all copied. So is a real barcode from a genuine pack.
    • Check-digit arithmetic can be recomputed by a counterfeiter who bothers. A failed check digit is close to conclusive; a passing one proves almost nothing.

    05 · Expiry interpreter

    EXP 04/27 is two different dates.

    Nigerian packs print dates in at least six conventions, sometimes with no legend. The tool shows every plausible reading, names the safe one, counts the months left, and applies the hard rule: an out-of-date antibiotic, injection or ORS is not a “a little bit weaker” product.

    How to carry it to a PHC with no power

    Pack it

    • Conditioned (sweating) cold packs on top and at the sides, never only under the bottom.
    • A cardboard buffer between packs and vials — direct contact freezes the antigen.
    • Vials upright, upright, upright. Spacer filler so nothing tips.
    • Sealed box, taped, void space minimised; do not open to “look” en route.

    Move it

    • Know the hours your packed box holds: 24 h is normal for a good validated carrier, 6 h for a foam pot in March sun.
    • Shade, vehicle floor not boot lid, never the dashboard.
    • Include a thermometer or a VVM you can read on arrival.
    • Time the trip honestly and write it on the box.

    On arrival

    • Shake test: a frozen-then-thawed vaccine often looks normal. Shake against another vial — a rusty or powdery sound means discard.
    • Read the VVM before anyone else does, and record the stage.
    • Log the maximum temperature and the hours out of range.
    • Anything you cannot explain goes to quarantine with a label, not to a patient.

    Nothing in the sample matches. In a real recall list, a non-matching batch does not make your pack safe — notices are batch-specific and partial.

    Where real notices live

    NAFDAC’s published alerts and product recall notices, the Federal Medical Centre / teaching hospital pharmacy bulletins, and your state drug officer. This page links nothing and queries nothing.

    Batch matching is exact

    A notice names a batch or a batch range, not a product. “Same brand” is not the same as “affected”. Write the batch down before you throw the carton away.

    Keep the evidence

    Pack, carton, leaflet, receipt and the remainder of the batch at the outlet. A regulator can do something with a sealed sample and a receipt; it can do nothing with a description.

    Who to tell, in order

    1. The licensed pharmacist or superintendent at the point of sale. They can test the number on the register from the outlet, and they carry the legal duty to quarantine stock. Show them the score.
    2. Your state drug/health officer or the state pharmacist. They can inspect the outlet and lift the whole batch, not just your pack. Send the printed report plus the pack.
    3. NAFDAC’s report channel — the regulatory reporting/integrity number and the online complaint form on nafdac.gov.ng, plus the state office address for physical samples. Report even if you cannot prove anything: patterns are how operations start.
    4. The prescriber, if the product was prescribed. A substandard antimalarial changes what the next treatment should be.

    Before you hand anything over

    • Photograph the pack, carton faces, batch block and the seal damage.
    • Keep the receipt. If there is none, ask for one and note the seller’s name.
    • Keep the remainder sealed; do not “finish it to be sure”.
    • Write the time you suspect a patient already took a dose.

    10 · The maths, in the open

    Assumptions you can argue with.

    Weights change verdicts, so they are visible and editable. A pharmacist who thinks “invoice from a licensed outlet” matters more than the deboss can move its weight, and the score follows. Nothing here is secret sauce.

    Date conventions this tool assumes
    • Two-digit years resolve to 20YY when the value is 40 or lower, otherwise 19YY, and the choice is always stated.
    • A bare MM/YY is read as end-of-month, i.e. usable to the last day of that month, and the alternative DD/MM reading is still shown.
    • Where two readings are plausible, the earlier one is labelled safe. Ambiguity is resolved against use, not in favour of it, because the failure mode is a dead child rather than a wasted ₦1,800.
    • A date that has passed under any plausible reading, on an antibiotic, injection, ORS or vaccine, is a hard stop — no scraping the label, no “it still smells fine”.
    What this tool cannot do

    It cannot query the NAFDAC register, decode a manufacturer’s internal batch code, assay content, detect an under-dose that passes every visual check, or tell you that a pack is safe. It cannot see the pack — you are the sensor, and your honest answers are the limit of its accuracy. The reference table, recall rows and excursion budgets are curated illustrations for teaching and drafting, clearly labelled as such, not a data feed. If a pack is for a child, for malaria, for sepsis or for insulin: when in doubt, discard and go to a licensed pharmacist.

      11 · Limits and reporting

      If it matters, verify it somewhere else.

      NOT A VERIFICATION

      A valid shape is not authenticity

      Every format rule on this page is public, which means it is copyable. Use the checker to decide whether to ask, then confirm the registration on the official register with a pharmacist present.

      NOT A LAB

      Invisible failures exist

      Under-dosing, wrong API, heavy metals and microbial contamination are invisible at a counter and undetectable by any checklist. That is why reports matter more than scores.

      NOT A DATA SOURCE

      Sample content is illustrative

      The product reference table uses generic names; the recall table is invented fiction; price bands are indicative planning ranges. No real company is accused of anything here.

      “The pack that makes you feel suspicious is worth more to the system than the pack you silently throw away.”

      Report it with the batch number, the outlet and the receipt — that is the only version of this that helps the next buyer.
