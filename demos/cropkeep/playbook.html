Playbook · for whoever holds the crate

Most of this is free.
Do it before you buy anything.

The six handling credits in the calculator between them take tomato from 40% loss to 8%, and not one of them costs money: cool hours, shade, crates, grading, a covered load, and unloading out of the sun. The hardware is the second decision, never the first.

    01 · Cut to cash

    Nine steps, in this order

    Each step either buys you shelf days or costs you none. Read the days-to-market number at step one again after every step: it is the figure everything else is measured against.

    1. Write the batch down before you cut

      Kilograms, farm-gate price, how many days to the buyer, and the honest temperature where it sits between the ridge and the road. That is the whole arithmetic of the day.

      The calculator's worked example is this list, applied to 1,000 kg of tomato at ₦180/kg.

    2. Harvest in the cool hours, before 10am

      Midday cutting leaves the field heat in the fruit, and field heat is the fastest rot accelerator on this tool. A crate of tomato picked at noon is already dying faster than one picked at 7am.

      Worth 5 points, and the only credit that costs nothing but discipline.

    3. Shade it within thirty minutes of cutting

      Not an hour, not after the next row. The crop has to leave the sun before the sun leaves its mark on it.

      Worth 6 points. Under a tree is shade; a sack thrown over the load is not.

    4. Grade and de-leaf at the farm, not at the market

      Damaged fruit and loose leaves are mould starters, and they travel to the buyer at your cost to be rejected at the gate.

      Worth 4 points, and it is also the sorting you must not postpone.

    5. Into crates, filled to the shoulder, stacked with air gaps

      The crate earns the largest single handling credit in the tool. Overfill it and you are back to the sack: the bottom row crushes and the heat has nowhere to go.

      Worth 8 points. Do not buy hardware you cannot fill properly.

    6. Cover the load and leave vents

      Sun, dust and wind scorch the top layer of an open bed, and the load arrives hotter than it left. Cover it, and stack so air can move.

      Worth 6 points.

    7. Unload into shade at the destination

      The last hour on the quayside in full sun is the most expensive hour of the trip. Get the crates out of the vehicle and under cover before anyone negotiates.

      Worth 4 points, completing all six credits.

    8. Decide hold or sell against margin days

      Shelf life at the temperature it actually sits at, minus the days the sale needs, is your margin. Positive means you can wait for a better price; near zero means you sell today at whatever the gate offers.

      That figure is the shelf-life check in the calculator, not a feeling.

    9. Weigh what the buyer rejected and log it

      Enter the actual loss in the ledger. The gap between what this tool projected and what you measured is the only number that teaches you anything about your own ridge.

      Three logged batches and the published baseline stops being yours.

    02 · What raises the loss

    Eight traps, and the money each one takes

    • Harvesting at midday. Because the truck cannot wait. The fruit leaves the field hot, and every hour it stays hot is spent off the shelf life before a buyer sees it.
    • Carrying the field heat into the load. Shade after cutting is a step, not a preference. A crop cooled in shade for an hour behaves like a different batch in the calculator.
    • Overfilled crates. Heaped to the brim and the shoulder row is crushed and sealed. You paid for crates and are running sacks with handles.
    • Sorting after transport instead of before. Damaged fruit travels at your cost, spreads mould to its neighbours, and is rejected at the gate where the price is worst. Grade on the farm.
    • Drying maize on a bare floor. Sand in the grain, re-wetting at night, mould at the edges of the spread. The tool's solar tray dryer exists at ₦145,000 for exactly this job.
    • Storing cassava like maize. Cassava deteriorates from within in 48 - 72 hours. No dry store, no bag and no shade stops that clock; only processing does.
    • Cooling a batch that arrived hot. A refrigerated van loaded in full sun spends most of its benefit pulling field heat out on the road. Cool it first, then move it.
    • Chilling a tomato below 10 °C. Pitted skin and lost flavour. The ladder flags chilling injury for a reason, and colder is not automatically better.

    03 · The harvest morning

    The checklist, before the first crate is filled

    Ninety seconds on the ridge. Every line here is one of the six handling credits, or the arithmetic that makes them worth claiming.

    • It is before 10am and the sun is not on the plot yet.
    • Shade is ready where you cut, not where you finish.
    • Crates are clean, dry and in the shade, not in the sun behind the store.
    • Nobody is filling a crate above the shoulder line.
    • Grading and de-leafing happens here, into a reject heap, not into the load.
    • The cover is on the vehicle and the stack keeps its vents.
    • You can say the weight, the gate price, the days to the buyer and the temperature out loud.
    • Only the steps you genuinely did are ticked in the calculator.

    04 · The days after cutting

    What to do on each day, perishables first

    Shelf days are a decision calendar. These are the points at which the answer changes, taken from the shelf-life figures in the crop table at the temperature a Nigerian yard actually sits at.

    Hour 0 - 2

    Cut, shade, grade, crate

    All six handling credits are earned in this window. Nothing you buy later recovers a hot afternoon.

    Day 0

    Settle the hold-or-sell question

    Compare shelf life at your temperature against the days the sale needs. If the margin is under half the trip, sell today.

    Day 1

    The trip is the whole risk

    Covered, vented, unloaded into shade. Leafy greens and tomato are already at the end of their life at 28 °C.

    Day 2

    Cassava must be processed

    The 48 - 72 hour internal clock is up. Paste, gari or flour now, or the tubers are a loss whatever they were packed in.

    Day 3

    Pepper, garden egg and mango are still sellable

    About 6.5 days of pepper life at 29 °C, 4.9 for garden egg, 4.9 for mango. Check the stems and the skin, and price before the buyer does.

    Day 5 - 10

    Only cooled, slow or clean lots survive

    Plantain at 10 days, pepper at 8, potato at 35 - and only if the chain held. Anything warmer than the band is running its own race.

    Week 1 - 2 (grain)

    Dry to safe moisture, then seal

    Maize, rice, groundnut and soybean are drying decisions now, not storage decisions. Hermetic bags go on dry grain only.

    Before sale (grain)

    Test, do not assume

    Aflatoxin in maize and groundnut is a laboratory finding. Weight and weevil damage are what this tool can price.

    05 · Read the batch

    Signs this lot will not reach the market intact

    Each sign below corresponds to a failure the tool already models. If you see two of them, treat the sale as a same-day decision at whatever price the gate offers.

    • Warm crates when you close them. Field heat still in the load. At 28 °C a tomato batch has 2.5 days, and the hot ones are burning through them faster.
    • Wet leaves and fruit mixed into the stack. Free moisture plus a closed stack is stem mould on pepper and botrytis on soft fruit.
    • Pulp or juice at the bottom of a sack. Crush, then rot at the bruise. Garden egg and tomato both start there and finish at the gate.
    • One ripe finger on a plantain bunch. Ethylene: the whole bunch follows it. Separate the fast bunches from the load or sell them first.
    • Cassava tubers going pink or streaked inside. Primary deterioration has started. Process now; it will not store.
    • Condensation on the inside of a covered load at dawn. The venting failed. Damp plus warmth is the mould recipe for mango and pepper.
    • A maize lot that smells musty or feels soft in the bag. Above about 13% moisture the aflatoxin risk rises. That is a laboratory question, and it is not a sale you should push.
    • Your margin days are negative in the calculator. The trip needs more days than the crop has. Cool it or shorten the journey; there is no third option on this page.

    06 · Questions farmers and aggregators ask

    Straight answers, no hedging

    Are these loss figures measured on my farm?

    No. They are mid-points of published post-harvest ranges for each crop in Nigerian conditions, and they move with season, variety, altitude, ridge position and who did the picking. Treat them as a starting hypothesis: log three batches, enter the actual loss after the sale, and use your own baseline from then on.

    Why can I not get loss below a certain number?

    Every crop in the tool has a floor, the best handling alone reaches: 8% on tomato, 15% on leafy greens, 3% on maize. Bruising in the last load, road vibration and a buyer who inspects badly are not fixed by care. A tool that promises you 0% with crates and shade is selling you a story.

    Charcoal cooler or CoolBot cold room?

    At around 1,000 kg a cycle the ₦190,000 cooler pays back in a handful of cycles and needs no power. The ₦950,000 room needs volume and a supply that holds, and the calculator throws it out entirely when you answer no reliable power. Change the tonnage and watch the top three reorder - that reordering is the point.

    Is the evaporative chamber worth it?

    In harmattan air, yes: ₦120,000 and about 12 °C off the ambient. In humid wet-season air it loses most of its advantage, which is why the tool gives it a weaker achieved loss than the charcoal cooler.

    What about aflatoxin in stored maize?

    Hermetic storage suppresses mould by limiting moisture and oxygen, and the calculator can price the weight you keep. It cannot tell you whether the lot is safe. That is a laboratory result and nothing here substitutes for it.

    Do I need crates if I sell the same day?

    You need the handling far more than the hardware, and the tool scores it that way: when days to a buyer is one or less, every storage option is marked down 25 points because a machine cannot earn its keep inside a morning. Shade, crates and speed still count.

    Why does the ranking change with my budget?

    An option costing more than twice your stated cash is excluded outright, and one whose seasonal cost alone exceeds it loses 18 points. That is a deliberate design: a technology you cannot afford this season is not a recommendation.

    What does a panic week mean in the planner?

    It is the week your volume beats the capacity of the storage you selected. That surplus has to go to market in the same week and takes the price down with it. The fix is more capacity or a spread-out pick, not a brave face at the gate.

    07 · Words this tool uses

    Glossary

    Every term the calculator writes on the screen, in the order you meet it.

    Field heat
    The heat the crop carries out of the field because it was cut in the sun and not shaded. It keeps cooking the batch inside the crate, and it is the fastest rot accelerator in this tool.
    Baseline loss (unimproved)
    The percentage this projection starts from: the mid-point of published post-harvest loss ranges for your crop in Nigerian conditions. Editable, and it should be your own weighed figure.
    Published range
    The low and high of that literature range, shown so you can see the baseline is a mid-point and not a measurement.
    Floor
    The lowest loss handling alone can reach for a crop, without cooling. Below it the loss is road, bruising and market handling, not carelessness.
    Shelf days
    How many days the batch still has sellable life at the temperature it actually sits at, computed from the crop's base shelf life by the Q10 rule.
    Margin days
    Shelf days minus the days your sale needs. Positive means you choose the day and the price. Near zero means the gate chooses both for you.
    Loss avoided
    The naira value of the percentage points a fix takes off the batch, per cycle and per season. It is the number every technology is ranked by.
    Payback
    What the fix costs divided by what it saves each cycle, expressed in harvest cycles and then in months at the season length you set. The cooler in the worked example pays back in 4.2 cycles.
    Q10
    The horticulture rule of thumb the tool uses: for every 10 °C you take off, the biochemical clock roughly halves and the shelf life roughly doubles. Above the crop's own limit it runs faster.
    Chilling injury
    Damage from being too cold, flagged on the shelf-life ladder. Tomato, banana and garden egg are sensitive; below roughly 10 to 13 °C more cold costs you quality.
    Perishable and dry
    The two classes in this tool. Perishables lose water and colour in days and are helped by cooling; dry goods lose weight to weevils, moisture and mould over months and are helped by hermetic and raised storage.
    Panic week
    A week in the planner where your volume beats the capacity of the option you selected, so the surplus goes to market immediately and drags the price down.
    Weighed actual
    The loss you enter into the ledger after the sale. The gap between it and the projected loss is the audit, and it is the figure that replaces the published baseline.
