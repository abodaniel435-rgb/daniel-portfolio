Playbook · before the letter arrives

A plan that still works
when the salary is late.

Income arrives. It simply arrives two or three weeks after the school expects it. That gap is what a sinking fund, a dated plan and one acknowledged letter are for. This is the order to do it in, and the eight places families lose the plot.

10 steps from the letter to a date the school has accepted8 traps how a plan quietly stops working8 documents to demand before you hand over cash

On this page

    01 · The sequence

    Ten steps, in this order

    Do them in order and the money is decided before the panic arrives. Do them out of order and you will be negotiating a number you never funded.

    1. Get the schedule in writing

      Ask for the fee schedule for the session and the instalment policy, before you save a naira. You are buying three things: the figure, the dates, and what the figure excludes.

      If a school will not put the fees in writing, the number is not finished yet.

    2. Cost each child separately

      Name, class, band, the fees line, then each of the extras: the five charged every term and the three charged once a year. Three children are three bills, not one bill divided by three.

    3. Write the three dates, not the total

      Admission day, about four weeks later, about nine weeks later. A total tells you what you owe; dates tell you when your money has to be ready. Build them in section 06 and the plan recalculates every payment against them.

    4. Work backwards to a monthly amount

      The bill less what you have already set aside, divided by the months left until the date. A ₦240,000 term in eight months is ₦30,000 a month. The same term in three weeks is not a saving problem at all.

    5. Segregate the fund

      A separate account, a target savings, or a welfare fund that only fees touch. Money that sits in the account your market list comes out of is not a fund; it is a habit you have not tested.

    6. Set the transfer for the day you are paid

      Not the day the letter comes, and not resumption week. If payday moves, the standing instruction does not have to move with it.

    7. Price the fallback before you need it

      Put your own bill through the comparison: the fund, paying from income, an advance at 10 or 15% a month, an instalment plan with or without a surcharge, and a circle whose turn falls in week six. You are deciding, in a calm month, what you will do in an ugly one.

    8. Ask for the dates in writing before term opens

      Send the instalment letter with the first payment you can genuinely make, and ask for an acknowledgement. A stamped copy, a signed page, or even a text reply with a date in it. That reply is the difference between a plan and an excuse.

    9. When the salary is late, move the plan before you borrow

      Use the fund as the bridge, not a 15% advance. Tell the school the same week, in writing, with the new date and the amount you are bringing. Then rebuild the plan in section 06 so the next deadline does not arrive unannounced.

    10. Close each term, then reopen the fund

      Collect the statement of account, tick every payment you have made, keep the receipts with it, and start the next term's saving in the same week. The rise announced in July is only survivable if the fund never fully closed.

    02 · How a plan quietly stops working

    Eight traps, and what each one costs

    • The fund is not segregated. Money in the salary account has already been promised to the market, the generator and the landlord. Two hard weeks and the fund is empty while the written plan still looks intact.
    • 'The school will wait'. Somebody will, but it will not be the person holding your file on examination week. Waiting is a decision made on a date you were not consulted about, and it shows up as a child sent home.
    • Two children's terms booked against one balance. One fund is fine; one promise per child is what matters. If the same ₦240,000 is counted twice before admission day, the second child pays the shortfall, and it is always the younger one's class that gets held.
    • An ajo circle running on a different clock. A circle pays in a fixed turn order. If your turn falls in week six and fees are due in week four, the circle did not fail you, the arithmetic did. The tool's own coverage figure assumes each member misses independently, and real circles miss together, because one late salary is three late members.
    • Counting the advance as a small monthly charge. 15% of ₦80,000 is ₦12,000 for thirty days, and that is the number you are shown. Rolled every month it is 435% a year, and the repayment each month is taken from the money the next term's fund needed.
    • The once-a-year items never enter the monthly figure. Uniform, books and PTA dues are annual charges. Leave them out of the monthly amount and they all land in the first week of the first term, on top of the admission payment, the bus and the ICT levy.
    • A dated plan you have not funded. A date the school has written down and you have missed costs more than no date at all: it spends the bursar's patience and your own leverage before the money is even gone.
    • No paper, no receipts, no statement. Cash at the gate with a name in a notebook leaves you arguing from memory about a figure you cannot see. Every payment buys a receipt with the term and the line items on it, or it did not happen.

    03 · Before you hand over cash

    The paperwork to demand first

    None of this is hostile. A school that plans fees properly has all of it already, and one that does not is telling you something about September.

    • The fee schedule for the session, in writing, with the due dates on it. Including what each line covers and what the fees figure does not.
    • The instalment policy as a figure, not a phrase. How many payments, on which dates, and whether there is a surcharge. Ask, then type the truth into the comparison.
    • A receipt for every payment with the child's name, the term and the line items. Not 'booked' in a class teacher's notebook.
    • A stamped or signed acknowledgement of your instalment letter. If they will not accept the letter, send it as a text or an email the same day so there is a dated record.
    • The school's own fees and examinations policy. Ask plainly what it withholds a child from, and over exactly what figure.
    • The refund rules in writing: caution fee, boarding balance, unused bus weeks. The caution fee is refundable in principle, and principles need a document.
    • A statement of account at the end of each term. What was paid, what it covered, what remains. Keep it with the receipts, in one folder.
    • One named person: the bursar, not only the class teacher. Teachers are often instructed to enforce. Bursars can usually agree dates.

    04 · Red flags on a fee bill

    Read the bill before you read the total

    Any one of these is a question to ask in August rather than a debt to discover in November.

    • A total with no line items. You cannot plan around a number you cannot break down, and you cannot argue with one either.
    • Tuition and a levy on the same line. Then nobody has to say which of the two rose this session.
    • 'As agreed', with no date and no figure. An agreement with no date is a favour, and favours are recalled on examination week.
    • The word reviewable with no review point. Ask what it is reviewed against, and who decides.
    • A discount that vanishes the moment you ask for instalments. That is a charge for the dates, so ask for it as a figure and price it properly.
    • Cash only, and no receipt offered until you ask. A school that cannot issue a receipt for ₦96,000 has a bookkeeping problem you will inherit.
    • A figure that changed since the letter, with no new letter. Ask for the revised schedule in writing before you pay the difference.
    • An item that was free last session and is charged this session. Usually a levy voted at a meeting. Ask which one and when it was decided.
    • Your child's name spelled differently on the bill and the receipt. Small, until the two documents are the evidence in a dispute about a balance.

    05 · The termly rhythm

    What to do, and when it is worth doing

    A term is about fourteen teaching weeks and three payment dates. Everything below is timed against those three dates.

    Month 1

    The fund opens

    On the day you are paid, before the market list. The transfer is what makes it a plan; the intention is not.

    Month 2

    Cost all the children

    Each child's bill, each term's dates, and the monthly amount the household needs. Then check whether that amount is the one you can actually save.

    Pre-term

    Confirm with the bursary

    Ask for the resumption date, the deadline and the instalment policy in writing. The plan builder asks how many days before each date you will confirm; five is the default for a reason.

    Admission

    The first payment

    The 40%, handed over with a receipt naming the child, the term and the amount. Tick it on the saved plan the same day.

    Week 4

    Mid-term payment

    The 30%, and the right moment to ask whether anything on the bill has changed. Do not discover a new levy in examination week.

    Week 9

    Before the exams

    The closing 30%. This is the last date a school treats as a negotiation rather than a rule.

    Term end

    Statement, then reopen

    Collect the statement of account, check it against every ticked line, and start the next term's saving in the same week.

    06 · Questions parents actually ask

    Straight answers, no hedging

    How much notice do I really need?

    Six months or more and the tool calls the deadline beatable: the monthly amount is usually one you can name. Under about three weeks, saving cannot rescue it, and the letter in section 07 becomes the plan instead.

    Can a school keep my child from examinations over fees?

    It happens, which is why you move first with money in hand and a dated letter. The calculator lists the steps to try, in order, and says plainly that it is not legal advice and cannot read your state's education law for you.

    Is a monthly salary advance ever the right answer?

    As a bridge measured in weeks, when the fund will repay it before the next instalment date, and when you have read the compounded figure rather than the monthly one. As a habit it costs more in a year than the fees it was borrowed to pay.

    Should I run one fund for three children or three?

    One account is fine. One promise per child is what matters. Name each child's dates and amounts first, then add them to get the monthly figure, and check the admission week specifically, because three children resuming in one week is three payments landing at once.

    What if I save and still fall short?

    Then the shortfall column is doing its job: it tells you what is still owed on the due date and translates it into weeks of the term unfunded. That number is the exact thing to negotiate before term starts, in writing, with a receipt.

    Do schools charge for paying in instalments?

    Many add nothing for two or three payments; some add 3-5%. Ask for it as a figure, type it into the comparison and let the ranking speak. A surcharge is not automatically unfair, it is just no longer free.

    Are PTA and development levies the same as fees?

    No. Fees go to the school; a levy goes to an association or a project, and is often voted at a meeting. Ask which line of your bill is which, because the two are renegotiated on different timetables.

    The circle has worked for years. Why plan around it?

    Because it pays on its own turn order rather than your deadline, and because one member's late salary becomes three members' missed week. Keep the ledger, keep it visible to everyone, and do not let it be your only plan.

    What interest should I assume on the fund?

    Zero, until your bank confirms a rate in writing. Target-savings and staff welfare funds often pay very little, and a plan built on a rate nobody quoted will leave you short in the one month it matters.

    07 · Words on a fee letter

    Glossary

    The vocabulary the calculator and your bursar both use, in the order it becomes relevant.

    Sinking fund
    A sum set aside every month, at a fixed amount, for one named debt. The deposit is the bill divided by the months left until the date, so the money arrives before the deadline rather than after it.
    Cover ratio
    What your saving will actually reach by the due date, as a percentage of the bill. At 100% the fund covers the term; at 78% you are negotiating the other 22% with the bursar.
    Term due date
    The day the school says the bill must be clear, usually written as on or before resumption. It is a date, not a week, and it is the only date the plan is built around.
    Part payment (40 / 30 / 30)
    The shape most Nigerian bursars will accept in writing: 40% on admission, 30% about four weeks later, 30% before the examinations.
    Instalment interest
    What a school or a lender charges for the dates rather than the money. A surcharge on the bill, or a monthly rate on the balance you still owe. Both are annualised in the comparison, because one of them always looks small.
    Refundable caution fee
    A deposit held against damage, returned under rules the school sets. Refundable in principle only; the writing is what makes it real.
    Once-a-year extras
    Uniform, books and PTA dues. Charged once, and spread across the terms by the planner so the monthly figure is honest.
    Teaching weeks
    The weeks a term actually sits, about fourteen. A shortfall divided by teaching weeks is the number of weeks of school your money has not paid for.
    Forgone return
    What the lump sum would have earned in your shop, your stock or your business cycle while it was locked into a paid term. Shown, not scolded.
    Pot and turn
    In an ajo or esusu circle, the weekly pool and the week you receive it. Your turn is either before the fee date or it is useless.
    Gap risk
    The chance a member misses a week. Coverage is calculated on the assumption that members miss independently; households and salaries miss together.
    Arrears
    The part of the bill past its agreed date. In a circle it is a name on a list; on a fee account it is a child kept from a paper.
    Statement of account
    The school's own written summary of what you paid, what it covered and what remains. Ask for it at the end of each term and keep it with the receipts.
    Monthly equivalent
    The year's fees divided by twelve. The number that turns a termly lump sum into something a salary can actually carry.

    08 · What no plan fixes

    Three honest limits

    A school that will not agree dates

    The plan cannot make a bursar accept an instalment, and no tool can know their policy. Ask, get the answer in writing, and let the comparison price the option that is left.

    Fees that vary by street

    Public, low-cost, standard, premium: these are national shapes, and by state, by city and by street they move. Your written schedule is the only figure that binds your plan.

    A plan is not a loan

    Nothing here lends, brokers or moves money. The advance and surcharge rates illustrate the products parents are actually shown at the gate and on the phone. Confirm any rate in writing before you sign.
