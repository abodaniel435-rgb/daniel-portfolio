00 · Why the fare is always wrong

Everyone quotes a seat price. Almost nobody can say what it covers.

The driver quotes from the park. The rider haggles from last year. Neither is wrong — but the number that leaves the motor park usually forgets wear, the return leg, the levies, and the fact that the person alighting at Ogba used a tenth of the trip the person going to Ibadan used.

A · the silent gap

0/ seat

What a seat costs, minus what a seat is sold for

On the default Benin → Lagos Sienna trip, one of six seats carries ₦14,987 of cost. At the ₦9,000 the park quotes, the trip is ₦35,922 short before the driver pays for anything. The gap is not greed — it is uncounted lines.

B · the unequal seat

0

Equal splits punish the short rider

Split the money evenly and the person riding 40 km pays the same as the one riding 245 km. RiderPool prices every seat by the distance actually travelled and shows how far each method drifts from that.

C · the free detour

0

A diversion has a price, and someone pays it

Eleven extra kilometres to drop someone inside a estate is fuel, wear and 22 minutes shared by everyone still in the car. The stop optimiser puts a number on it and flags the stops that cost more than they bring.

01 · How this works without an app

There is no pool to join. There is a spreadsheet that argues honestly.

This is a static page on GitHub Pages. It cannot see other people, cannot ping a server, cannot hold your money and cannot tell you who is boarding at Oba. Real pooling happens on a phone call and a WhatsApp thread; what a tool can do is make the numbers in that thread checkable. Here is the whole workflow:

  1. One person — driver or organiser — fills the trip builder on this page. Twelve inputs, no account.
  2. The fare maths, the four split modes and the stop economics compute live in their browser.
  3. They press Copy trip card and paste the plain-text card into the WhatsApp group, or print the manifest and carry it to the park.
  4. Interested riders reply with their own numbers — who boards where, who alights where, who has luggage — and the organiser types those in and re-sends. That round trip is the matching layer. It is deliberately a human conversation.
  5. Money changes hands in person, as it already does. The page records who has paid only so the organiser's ledger matches reality; nothing is transferred, escrowed or verified.
  6. Before departure, both sides work the printed checklists. The six-character trip code is an agreed reference for that conversation — a checksum on a shared piece of paper, not a booking number.

What it never claims to be

  • Not a transport company: it owns no vehicles and employs no drivers.
  • Not a marketplace: the published-trips board is clearly labelled sample data that ships with the page.
  • Not an insurer or verifier: it checks no licence, no roadworthiness and no identity.
  • Not a payment rail: no gateway, no wallet, no refund mechanism.

Where the numbers come from

Corridor distances, fuel prices, vehicle economy and every levy on this page are editable reference tables with an "as of" date you set. There is no live feed — static hosting cannot have one. Update the table when the pump or the park changes, and the whole tool follows.

Where your data lives

Only in this browser's localStorage: trips, rider names, phone numbers, your own travel log. Export as CSV, print, or clear it in one click. Nothing is uploaded because there is nowhere to upload it to.

04 · Who pays what

Four ways to split the same money. Only one is honest about distance.

This is the argument at every motor park. Enter who boards where and who alights where, then compare the four methods people actually use. The yardstick is cost causation — each rider's share of the distance consumed — and the fairest method is the one whose payments drift least from it while still collecting the whole cost.

The ±45 minute window is the number you read to a rider on the phone: "be at the park between 6:15 and 7:00". Widen it if your park is chaos; narrow it if your riders are punctual.

How each method is scored

Cost causation is the yardstick: a rider's fair share is the whole trip cost multiplied by the kilometres they ride, divided by the sum of everyone's kilometres. For every method we then measure how far each rider's payment drifts from that share, and whether the method collects the full cost. Low drift with a full collection is the honest answer; the rest are conventions.

The driver's cut, tolls and levies are treated as common costs inside the pool — they do not vary by rider. A method that recovers less than the total is not fair, it is a loss.

Worked example, start to finish

Benin City → Lagos in a Sienna: 245 km at 9 km/L, ₦1,650 a litre, ₦49/km wear, ₦5,000 tolls, ₦3,000 levies, ₦25,000 to the driver, six seats on sale at ₦9,000. Recomputed live from whatever you have configured.

    10 · Honest limits

    What this tool refuses to pretend.

    Nobody is verified

    No driver, no identity, no vehicle check

    RiderPool has never seen the car, the licence, the insurance or the tyre. The checklists exist because the humans in the trip are the only verification layer that has ever worked on these roads.

    No matching

    It cannot find you a rider

    There is no pool, no queue, no notification. The sample board is fiction written into the source file. Real pooling is a phone call, and this page's job is to make the numbers in that call checkable.

    No money moves

    Not a payment system

    Nothing is charged, held, escrowed or refunded. "Mark paid" is a pencil tick in your own browser; if the phone dies on the way to the park, your ledger dies with it unless you exported it.

    Costs are estimates

    Fuel, wear and levies are yours to correct

    Economy figures are averages, not measurements of your engine. Union levies, "orientation" charges and barrier demands vary by park and by day. Where the table says ₦3,000 and the agent says more, the table is wrong and you should edit it.

    Safety is not arithmetic

    A fair fare has never prevented a crash

    Overloading, night driving and worn tyres are decisions, not numbers. The tool makes the cost of an extra body visible; it cannot stop the extra body from boarding.

    Legality sits elsewhere

    Routing, permits and fare caps are local

    What a park union permits, what your route requires and what you may charge are decided by state law and local practice, not by this calculator. It prices honestly. It does not advise.

    • The ETA chain assumes the average speed you type and the buffer you set. A bus lane, a toll queue or a flooded section will beat both.
    • Distance-based splitting is only as good as the stop kilometres you enter; "245 km" typed by memory moves every rider's share.
    • Break-even counts seats, not legal seats. If the class says six and eight bodies board, the maths is right and the trip is not.
