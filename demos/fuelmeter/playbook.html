Playbook · for the person who pays for the fuel

You do not have a cost
per kilometre. You have a guess.

The guess is usually the fuel figure, rounded down, measured from a gauge that lies, in a car with tyres nobody has checked since the last hole. This is the sequence that turns it into a number you can price a job on, defend to a passenger and re-measure next month.

    02 · The odometer method

    Ten steps to a km/L figure nobody can argue with

    The method is old and it is boring, which is why almost nobody does it. It needs a pump that displays litres, an odometer you photograph, and two visits to the same station. Do it once and you know your car; do it monthly and you know when your car changes.

    1. Pick one vehicle and one station

      The log groups samples by vehicle, so a keke and a Corolla in the same list stay separate but teach you nothing together. Pick the vehicle you are trying to price and the station you actually pass.

      Same station, same nozzle if you can. Different meters disagree.

    2. Fill until the nozzle cuts off twice

      That is your full. Do not squeeze another half litre in to make the number round, because the rounding is one of the lies further down this page.

    3. Zero the trip meter, or note the odometer

      You need a distance start point. The odometer reading is what the tool asks for, so write it down as you stand at the pump, not in the car ten minutes later.

    4. Drive normally for one full tank

      Not your best week and not a special trip. Your ordinary mixed life: commute, errands, one go-slow you could not avoid. Fuel used for a trip you did not count is the classic error.

    5. Return to the same station, same nozzle if possible

      It removes the meter difference between stations from the comparison, which is otherwise invisible in your figures.

    6. Fill the same way again

      Until the nozzle cuts off twice. Do not top up after that. The litres you are about to buy are almost exactly the litres you used.

    7. Write all four numbers

      Date, litres to two decimals, the naira actually paid, and the odometer now. The tool stores all four plus a full-tank tick and a short note.

      Tick full tank on both entries. Unticked rows never produce a km/L sample.

    8. Enter the litres, not the naira

      Litres is the field that becomes km/L. Money is the field that becomes your average per litre. Filling only the money column means you can never compute the number you came here for.

    9. Read the learned figure, then price jobs on it

      Two consecutive full-tank rows give one sample: distance divided by litres. The learned panel shows it against the claimed figure and against your own first readings, which is the baseline.

    10. Repeat monthly and watch the trend, not the sample

      One pair is an anecdote. Six pairs is a line. When the line drops more than 12% below your baseline, the tool flags the usual causes: under-inflated tyres, old oil, a dragging brake, a fouled filter.

    03 · The traps

    Seven habits that make your cost per kilometre a lie

    None of these are arithmetic errors. The maths is fine in every case. Each one is a measurement habit that feeds the calculator a number that is not true, and each one points in the same direction: your cost looks lower than it is.

    04 · Cost leaks

    Eight places the money goes, and what each one touches

    A leak is not a fault. It is a decision you are making repeatedly, usually without a figure attached to it. The penalties quoted here are the calculator's own editable factors.

    • Traffic with the engine running. The idle rate is 0.6 L per hour on petrol, four fifths of that on diesel. An hour a day of engine-on waiting is a visible line in your monthly litres, and the departure time that avoids it is the cheapest discount on any road.
    • Hard acceleration from every light. It is not in the multiplier table, which is the problem: your learned km/L in city traffic is where it shows. The go-slow row is a multiplier, not a driving licence.
    • A/C you do not need. The default A/C penalty is 8% more burn. Use it when the cabin is full or the sun has baked the dashboard, and open a window on a cool morning instead. Both numbers are yours to edit.
    • Weight you carry for no reason. The load penalty is 2.5% per 100 kg. A boot of tools, spare jerrycans and old stock is permanent fuel cost, and on a diesel pickup the same weight is also shortening the tyre set.
    • Fuel bought at the dear end of your corridor. The shipped table is ₦80 per litre wide, so a 50 litre fill is up to ₦4,000 apart depending where you stop. On Kano to Bauchi or Abuja to Kaduna, where you fill is a decision, not an accident.
    • Alignment you keep postponing. An alignment and balancing job is the cheapest row on the servicing table, and skipping it is what makes a tyre set reach half of the life you amortised. The cost lands twice: in litres and in the tyre line.
    • Skipping the service to save the service money. Servicing sits in your per-kilometre figure whether you pay it this month or next. Deferring it does not remove it, it just means you meet it later as a bigger invoice.
    • Empty seats you are paying for alone. The total per km does not care who is in the car. Cost per seat is the same money divided by more people; nothing about the vehicle gets cheaper, so fill the seats or accept the whole figure.

    05 · Before you set off

    The pre-trip checklist for a long one

    Ninety seconds at the car, and most of the expensive surprises on a Nigerian trip are either fixed or priced. Do this before you load the boot, not after.

    • Tyre pressures cold, against the door placard, plus the spare. The spare is the one nobody checks, and it is the one you need at 9pm in an unfamiliar town.
    • Oil and coolant levels, with the engine cold. A level that needs topping between services is telling you something, and the tell is cheaper than the repair.
    • The litres your tank actually holds, against the distance you must cover. Run the trip first. The planner prints how many fill-ups the distance is and how much fuel money to carry, which is a different figure from one tank.
    • Fuel money ready for the far end, where the price row is dearer. Check both states on the price table and plan which end you fill at.
    • Washer fluid, wipers, and every light working. Rain and a harmattan dust storm put the same demand on the same three things.
    • Jack, wheel brace, and a tyre you can actually drive on. And know which bolt holds it down. A good spare and no tool is still a stranded car.
    • The papers you will be asked for, in the glovebox. Not in the house, not in another phone, not in somebody's bag.
    • The odometer photographed before you move. So the return leg has a start point and the log entry has a real number instead of a memory.
    • The route distance typed in, and the road condition set honestly. If part of it is city stop-go for you, do not price it as open road. The multiplier is on consumption, not on distance.

    06 · Budgeting a month of it

    The monthly routine, in order

    A month of fuel is not a number you guess in December. It is a figure you rebuild the same way every month from the odometer, and the reason to do that is the pump row that moves without telling you.

    Day 1

    Start from the odometer, not the pump

    Read the car at the beginning of the month and subtract the reading from last month. That is your kilometres, and it is the only figure in the budget nobody can argue with you about.

    Litres bought tell you what you spent. Distance tells you what you got.

    Day 1

    Enter the real commute and the real weekend

    One-way kilometres, days a week, weeks a month, then the weekend trips separately. The blended consumption matters because weekend distance is usually open road and the commute is usually not.

    Day 2

    Set the buffer, and be honest about the idle hours

    The buffer line covers detours, errands and the trip you forgot. The idle hours line covers the traffic you cannot avoid. Both are cheap to enter and both stop the month ending in a surprise.

    Weekly

    Log every fill-up the day you buy it

    Litres, money, odometer, full-tank tick. Five fills a month produce the samples that make the learned km/L real, and they arrive only if you write them down at the pump.

    Mid-month

    Check how many litres you have left before the month closes

    The budget panel prints litres per month and how often you fill. If you are through two thirds of the month's litres by the fifteenth, the month is going to be worse than the estimate and you still have time to change the pattern.

    Month end

    Price the pump-rise rows, then decide

    The scenario lines show what +10%, +15%, +20% and +30% do to your own litres. Plan against the 20% row, not against today's price, because today's price is not a promise.

    Every month

    Compare fuel as a share of what you earn

    Enter earnings and the bar reads the honest number. Above a fifth of earnings the vehicle is fragile; above a third, no driving habit will save it and the vehicle class or the base is the conversation.

    07 · Questions drivers ask

    Straight answers, no hedging

    Why is my cost per kilometre higher than what the tool says for my car?

    Because the profile figure is an open-road number and your week is not open road. A Corolla at 12 km/L in the table is nearer 10.7 at the city multiplier and 9.6 in a go-slow, before you switch the A/C on or put 100 kg in the boot. Log two full tanks and the learned figure replaces all of that with your own.

    Should I price a commercial job on the total or the marginal figure?

    On the marginal figure if the insurance, registration and park levies are already paid and the vehicle sits idle anyway: that is fuel plus servicing, tyres and oil. On the total if the trip is what makes the vehicle earn at all, or if the kilometres you are pricing are replacing paid work. The tool prints both, separately, and the gap between them is the whole argument.

    How many fill-ups do I need before the learned km/L means anything?

    Two full-tank rows give one sample. Three samples let the trend line start, because the panel compares your last three readings against your first three. Anything under that is one week of luck or bad luck.

    The tool says my economy fell 14%. What should I actually do first?

    Tyre pressure, cold, against the door placard. It is free, it takes five minutes, and it is the usual answer. Then the oil and when it last changed, then a dragging brake, which you can feel as a wheel that is hotter than the others after a drive.

    Is it worth driving to the cheaper state to fill up?

    Sometimes. The spread in the shipped table is ₦80 per litre, so 50 litres is ₦4,000. If the detour costs you 20 km of your own per-kilometre figure and an hour of go-slow, you have bought the cheap fuel with the expensive litres. Put both versions in the planner and look at the difference instead of arguing.

    My gauge says full but the pump gives more. Which do I trust?

    The pump. The gauge reads a float in a tank with headspace above the fuel line, and needles drift. That is precisely why the method is fill to the cut-off, drive a tank, fill to the cut-off again, and divide the distance by the litres you bought the second time.

    Does ride-hail beat driving myself?

    On the shipped figures, a hail car is ₦230 per km for the whole vehicle: about ₦57.50 per km split four ways, against ₦181 per km total for the default Corolla or ₦134 if you only count fuel. Below roughly 11 km the ₦2,500 minimum is the whole fare and hail looks dearer than it is on a long trip. Solve your own distance in the head-to-head panel; that is what the crossover line is for.

    Should I include depreciation in the number?

    If you are pricing work that a new vehicle will have to replace, yes, and you will have to add it yourself: this tool does not model it and says so. Put your monthly figure in the fixed line if you want it amortised per kilometre, and keep it separate in your head when you read the result.

    08 · The vocabulary

    Words you will need to argue about a per-kilometre figure

    Most arguments about running costs are two people using the same word for different sums. These are the definitions the calculator applies.

    Fuel cost per km
    Pump price per litre divided by km/L, adjusted for your conditions. The line that ignores every other cost.
    Total cost per km
    Fuel plus servicing, tyres and oil amortised, plus insurance and park levies spread over the kilometres you really do. The number you should price work on.
    Marginal cost per km
    The total without the amortised fixed line. What one extra kilometre costs you when the fixed money is already gone.
    Cost per seat per km
    The same total divided by the people in the vehicle. It splits money, it does not reduce it.
    Wear per km
    Servicing plus the tyre set divided by its life plus the oil service divided by its interval. What the road charges you even when fuel is cheap.
    Amortised fixed cost
    Insurance, registration and park levies divided by a monthly distance. Drive less and this line rises, which is the fact nobody likes.
    Full-tank method
    Fill to the cut-off, drive a tank, fill to the cut-off again. The litres you bought the second time are the litres you used.
    Learned km/L
    Distance between two consecutive full-tank odometer readings divided by the litres bought at the second one. Your measurement, not the catalogue's.
    Baseline
    The average of your first three samples. The flag compares your recent readings to it, so it is your own past performance and not somebody else's brochure.
    Road multiplier
    The factor applied to consumption, not to distance: 1.00 open road, 1.12 city stop-go, 1.25 go-slow as shipped.
    Idle rate
    Litres an hour burned standing still, 0.6 on petrol by default and four fifths of that on diesel.
    Crossover distance
    The kilometres at which driving yourself and buying the trip cost the same per person. Below it one wins, above it the other, and the tool solves for it instead of guessing.
    One-tank test
    Litres needed divided by your tank capacity. Above 1.0 means the trip is more than one fill-up, so the fuel money you carry changes.
    As-of date
    The date you set on the price rows. It is not a live feed and never will be: you are the source, and the number is only as honest as the day you wrote it.

    09 · Where the method stops working

    Three honest boundaries

    One bad fill ruins a sample, quietly

    A short fill you recorded as a full one, a jerrycan bought for a generator, or a trip meter reset in the middle: any of these produces a km/L that looks plausible and is wrong. Note anything unusual in the note field while you still remember it.

    Your driving style is a variable, not a constant

    Two weeks of gentle commuting and one week of hard running in traffic are different cars as far as the log is concerned. Read the trend line over months and treat any single sample as an anecdote.

    Nothing here is a quotation

    Per-kilometre figures, fare rows and wear averages exist to give you a number to argue with. A union levy, a park agent's demand, a toll or an orientation charge is a conversation, and this tool does not model any of them.
