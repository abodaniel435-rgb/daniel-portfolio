Nobody prices per kilogram.
Everybody prices per crate.

This page is the arithmetic underneath the board: the unit weights that turn a price into a comparison, the tier prices that make a spread real, the seasonal shape that tells you which way the month is going, and the road costs that decide whether a trip pays at all. Every figure is editable on the board, and yours replace these the moment you type them.

    01 · The six numbers that drive every answer

    Change these and the whole tool changes with them

    The board, the haul calculator, the season panel and the fair-price band all read from the same small set of assumptions. They live in section 09 of the tool, and they are deliberately rounded: two significant figures, honestly.

    22 kgthe crate of tomato, wood included
    50 kgthe bag, for rice, maize, beans and onion
    ₦244/kmdefault one-way transport rate
    20%reference loss on a hot soft-crop haul
    4%reference loss on grain, and it is weevils and theft, not heat
    3.11×gross spread on tomato, Zaria gate to Mile 12, before one cost line

    02 · Units and what they really weigh

    The unit is the trick. Here is what each one is worth in kilograms.

    ₦1,100 a mudu can be dearer per kilogram than ₦26,000 a crate, and a 25 litre keg of palm oil is 22.7 kg of oil, not 25 kg. Weigh one unit of each once, keep the number, and every offer on the market becomes comparable. These are the weights the board plans with.

    Weights are rounded planning figures, the same ones the board uses. Where a commodity carries its own weight on the board, the commodity wins over the unit. Confirm by weighing or counting once, then type your number into section 09.

    04 · What each desk adds

    The same crate costs different money at different market gates

    Every market on the board carries a multiplier: rent, levy culture, porter habit, distance from the farm belt. The tool prices a commodity by taking its tier figure and scaling it by that desk's multiplier, so you can see the spread before you hire a truck. The tomato column is what ₦9,000 at the gate, ₦17,500 wholesale and ₦28,000 at retail becomes at that desk.

    Which way each crop group moves, and what to do about it

    Direction, not prediction. Each commodity carries its own lean month on the board and the index swings around it; this table is the same shape read across the whole year, so you can see whether the month you are standing in is giving you an edge or taking one away.

    Direction words only, on purpose. The number the tool works with is the commodity's own index: 1 + swing × cosine of the distance from its lean month, which is why the same month is a peak for yam and a glut for maize.

    07 · Making these yours

    Three edits that change every answer on this page

    Type this morning's prices into the board

    The reference rows are a starting shape with an as-of date. Change one farm-gate figure and the spread, the fair band and the break-even quantity all move with it, because they are the same numbers read three ways.

    Weigh the unit before you argue about the price

    A crate that comes to you at 19 kg of goods and 3 kg of wet wood is not the board's crate. Set the kilogram figure in section 09, or override it per commodity, and stop comparing labels.

    Replace the rate with your driver's quote

    ₦244 per kilometre is a planning default, not a market. A driver who quotes ₦190,000 for the Zaria trip and one who quotes ₦244 × 780 are quoting the same trip; a third who wants ₦260,000 is telling you something about fuel, return load or his own calendar.

    08 · Where to check these figures

    Ask for the dated table, not the website

    Real prices come from people who are at the market. This page exists so you can see what the tool assumed, not so you can avoid asking.

    • The market union or market women's association at the specific market you trade in, for the day's tier price and the levy schedule.
    • The commodity traders' association for your crop, for the crate, basket and mudu conventions that actually apply on that route.
    • The monthly public agricultural marketing price reports (the FMARD and NAICS series) and the price tables your state marketing board issues, asked for by date.
    • Your own trade log, exported as CSV from the record page. Twelve months of your own buys and sells is the only price history that describes your market.
    • One physical scale and one counted crate. Weigh twice, then trust the number you wrote down.
