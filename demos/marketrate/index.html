01 · The problem

Nobody prices per kilogram. Everybody prices per crate.

A farmer in Zaria sells 40 crates of tomato at the gate. A trader in Lagos would pay three times as much — but after a 780 km trip, two sets of levies, porters at the market gate and a fifth of the crop spoiled in the back of a truck in traffic, “three times as much” may be worth less than the gate price.

Aggregators in Akure price pepper by the bag while buyers quote per mudu. A garri seller at Bodija restocks on feel while inflation moves under her. A household in Abuja is asked ₦85,000 for a bag of rice and has nothing to compare it to. The unit is the trick, the spread is the trap, and the cost line is the thing nobody writes down.

WHO USES IT

Four seats at the same market

The smallholder with 40 crates, the pepper aggregator, the garri seller deciding whether to restock today, and the household buyer who just wants to know if the price is fair.

THE INVISIBLE LINE

Margin is not profit

A ₦19,000 per-crate spread survives only if the trip, the levies, the porters and the spoilage leave anything behind. This tool subtracts them in front of you.

THE UNIT TRAP

Crate, mudu, basket, bag

₦1,100 a mudu can be more expensive per kilogram than ₦26,000 a crate. Comparing anything else is guessing.

    Every number the tool invents is editable.

    The multiplier scales a commodity’s tier price for that market’s own cost of doing business: rent, levies, porter culture, distance from the farm belt. Seasonal index = 1 ± swing, peaking in the lean month.

    The formulas, written out
    • Per-unit contribution = (1 − spoilage) × sell price − buy price − porter fee.
    • Extra profit = quantity × contribution − (trip cost + levies). Baseline is selling everything at the origin price.
    • Break-even quantity = (trip cost + levies) ÷ contribution, rounded up, when contribution is positive.
    • Cost per unit sold = total outlay ÷ units that survive to be sold, so spoilage shows up as an inflated goods cost.
    • Store-to-sell profit = sell price × (1 − loss) − buy price − storage cost × months − buy price × financing × months.
    • Fair band = 25th–75th percentile of the tier price for that commodity across every desk sampled in that city, each scaled by that desk’s cost multiplier only. Your board is read as this week’s prices, so the month’s seasonal index is never applied twice — the season panel tells you which way the band is about to move instead.
    What this cannot do, and where real numbers come from

    This is a curated indicative dataset for planning, not a live price feed, and it is not a quotation service. Your edited prices always take precedence over the reference figures — that is the point of the board.

    Real prices come from people who are at the market: the market union or market women’s association at the specific market you trade in; the commodity traders’ association for your crop; the federal and state agricultural marketing price reports published each month (FMARD and NAICS series, and the commodity price tables the state marketing boards issue); and your own ledger of trades. Ask for the dated table rather than trusting any website, including this one.

    What it cannot do: predict tomorrow’s price, account for a road closure, an harvest failure, a border, a fuel increase, a union dispute, or a buyer with better information than you. It cannot tell you whether a specific seller is cheating you — only whether the number is inside the ordinary range.

      10 · Honest limits

      A planning sheet, not a market oracle.

      NOT A FEED

      Indicative, editable, dated

      Reference prices are a curated starting point with an “as of” date you set. They are not scraped, quoted or guaranteed, and they go stale on purpose so you replace them.

      NO FAKE PRECISION

      Two significant figures, honestly

      Spoilage, multipliers and seasonal swings are rounded planning shapes. Nothing here claims to know your market to the naira — the arithmetic is exact, the inputs are not.

      SOURCES, NOT CITATIONS

      Go and ask

      Market unions, traders’ associations, the monthly public agricultural price reports, and your own trade log. This tool deliberately names no source it cannot stand behind.

      “The trader who knows the per-kilometre cost of a trip is the one still trading in March.”

      Everything here is arithmetic you can do on paper. The tool’s only real value is that it makes you write the costs down before you commit the capital.
